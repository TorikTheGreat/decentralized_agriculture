\section{Food self-sufficiency as a computable quantity}\label{sec:nutrition-analysis}

Whether a food-production system can make its user self-sufficient in food cannot be stated in advance for every user. It depends on the crops grown, the growing area and daylight available, the local climate and the energy the user can supply, so it has to be computed for each configuration. The requirement side varies in the same way. Human energy requirements are calculated as the basal metabolic rate multiplied by a physical activity level (PAL), and they differ with sex, age, body weight and habitual activity. The FAO/WHO/UNU expert consultation states that its requirements apply to population groups, and that the requirement of a specific individual must be based on that person's own energy expenditure or on estimates that take their habitual activity into account \cite[Section~5.4.1, p.~40]{faowhounu2004energy}. Its tables give, for example, about 2{,}100~kcal per day for women aged 18 to 30 weighing 55~kg at a PAL of 1.60 \cite[Table~5.7, p.~44]{faowhounu2004energy}. For men of the same age, 1.69~m tall with a body mass index of 21 and a PAL of 1.75, they give about 2{,}800~kcal per day (11.7~MJ) \cite[Section~5.4.2, p.~40; Table~5.4, p.~41]{faowhounu2004energy}; the two examples differ in activity level as well as in body size. The EAT--Lancet Commission bases its reference diet on a population-level adult energy intake of about 2{,}400~kcal per day, which it estimated assuming a body mass index of 22 and an active level of physical activity \cite[p.~1633; Table~1, p.~1632]{rockstrom2025eatlancet}. The calculations in this section use 2{,}400~kcal per day and are repeated at 2{,}100 and 2{,}800.

This section estimates the range within which the per-configuration computation falls. It combines open-field yield statistics, published measurements of crops grown in controlled environments and published nutrient requirements, and it reports area and energy per person for each stated configuration rather than a single verdict. The estimates are for orientation only. Each one rests on yields measured elsewhere, under conditions that differ from any particular installation, and they are to be replaced by measurements from the project's own prototypes as these become available. Figures labelled ``our calculation'' are reproduced by the scripts in the project repository (\texttt{whitepaper/food\_data\_analysis/snut\_analysis}, with open-field yields from \texttt{playground/compute\_caloric\_yield.py}); simple ratios of figures quoted from a source are labelled ``our arithmetic''.

\subsection{The open-field baseline}\label{sec:nutrition-field}

The theoretical ceiling on photosynthesis is the starting point. Zhu, Long and Ort calculate that the maximum efficiency with which solar energy can be converted into biomass is 4.6\% for C3 photosynthesis and 6\% for C4 photosynthesis, at 30~\textdegree{}C and an atmospheric CO$_2$ concentration of 380~ppm \cite[Abstract, p.~153]{zhu2008maximum}. These figures are relative to the total incident solar energy and refer to total biomass, not to the harvested product \cite[Fig.~2, p.~155]{zhu2008maximum}, and their last step assumes that respiration consumes 30\% of the energy fixed, against 30 to 60\% reported for major crops \cite[p.~156]{zhu2008maximum}.% SECONDARY: the 30-60% range is from Amthor 1989 and Amthor 2000 (zhu2008 refs 18, 19).
 The C3 ceiling falls as temperature rises, while the modelled C4 ceiling does not; the C4 advantage shrinks as CO$_2$ rises and may disappear near 700~ppm, except at the highest temperatures \cite[Abstract, p.~153; Fig.~3, p.~156]{zhu2008maximum}, which matters for a tropical site: there the C3 ceiling is lower, and the C4 advantage persists even at elevated CO$_2$. Measured on the radiation the crop intercepts, the highest observed conversion efficiencies are, with few exceptions, about one third of the theoretical value \cite[p.~243]{zhu2010photosynthesis}.% SECONDARY: zhu2010 takes this from its ref. 13 (Beadle et al. 1985, UNEP).
 Zhu, Long and Ort also report, from field studies, that nitrogen fertiliser raises this efficiency in major cereals \cite[p.~243]{zhu2010photosynthesis}.% SECONDARY: refs 43 (Hall et al. 1995, sunflower) and 86 (Muurinen & Peltonen-Sainio 2006, spring cereals); neither covers rice.
 Separately from conversion efficiency, most crops do not use the whole period in which temperatures and water are adequate for growth \cite[pp.~238--239]{zhu2010photosynthesis}. Season length, nutrient supply and CO$_2$ are conditions that a controlled environment can change.

What fields deliver in practice can be computed from production statistics. Combining FAOSTAT yields for 2022 \cite{faostat2025qcl} with USDA food composition data \cite{usdafdc2018srlegacy} (our calculation), world-average open-field yields give about 2{,}070~kcal per square metre per year for maize and 1{,}720 for potato. Among countries harvesting more than 50{,}000~ha of the crop, the highest national averages are about 4{,}170 (maize, Spain) and 3{,}780 (potato, United States). Table~\ref{tab:nutrition-field} converts these into the area needed to supply one person's energy. The calculation counts one harvest per year, so for short-cycle crops in climates that allow several harvests it overstates the area.

\subsection{Buying area with light}\label{sec:nutrition-ce}

Controlled-environment research gives much higher yields per square metre of growing area, at a high input of light. NASA's Biomass Production Chamber provided 20~m$^2$ of growing area spread over four stacked shelves; crops were grown under high-pressure sodium or metal halide lamps or a mixture of the two, with CO$_2$ held at 1{,}000 or 1{,}200~ppm during the light period \cite[Section~2.1, pp.~707--708]{wheeler2008crop}. The highest edible productivity in the chamber was 18.4~g of potato dry matter per square metre per day at a daily light integral of 42.2~mol~m$^{-2}$~d$^{-1}$ \cite[Table~2, p.~711]{wheeler2008crop}, which corresponds to about 68~kcal per square metre per day (our calculation). In a run at the University of Wisconsin, in a contiguous stand of which only the centre plants were harvested, with a transplant scheme and continuous light for most of the cycle, potato reached 37.5~g of tuber dry matter per square metre per day at 52.4~mol~m$^{-2}$~d$^{-1}$ after adjusting for the smaller area used before transplanting, or 33.0~g without the adjustment \cite[p.~82; Table~1, p.~83; p.~84]{wheeler2006potato}. Tibbitts, Cao and Wheeler report the same productivity, convert it to 139~kcal per square metre per day and 20.1~m$^2$ for 2{,}800~kcal per day, assuming that the whole harvest is used and that nothing is lost in storage, and propose a growing area of 4.9 by 4.9~m (about 24~m$^2$) to allow for yield variation and storage losses \cite[p.~iv; p.~30]{tibbitts1994potato}. That run used 1{,}000~ppm CO$_2$, and the authors estimate that its lamps, at 551~W per square metre, would draw 11.1~kW continuously per person for 2{,}800~kcal per day \cite[pp.~28--31]{tibbitts1994potato}, or about 97~MWh per year (our arithmetic), at the lamp efficiency they assumed; with the LEDs considered below, the same yield would need about 29 to 36~MWh at 2{,}800~kcal per day (our calculation from Table~\ref{tab:nutrition-ce}). The best wheat yield reviewed here is 60~g of grain dry matter per square metre per day, averaged over a 79-day cycle at 150~mol~m$^{-2}$~d$^{-1}$ (reached with a 20-hour photoperiod) and 1{,}200~ppm CO$_2$ \cite[Abstract, p.~869; Table~I, p.~871]{bugbee1988limits}.

These are research results with conditions attached. The best potato run is a stand of 16 harvested plants in containers of peat and vermiculite \cite[pp.~28--29]{tibbitts1994potato}. In the same report, consistent tuber production was obtained only when solid media were used, in containers or as a thin layer in nutrient-film trays, and the best growth and tuber yields came from nutrient film over 2 to 3~cm of gravel or arcillite \cite[p.~iv; p.~6]{tibbitts1994potato}; under a 24-hour photoperiod, however, nutrient film over 1~cm of arcillite gave about a fifth of the tuber weight obtained in pots after 54 days \cite[Table~2.5, p.~17]{tibbitts1994potato}, and the authors conclude that additional work is needed to ensure high tuber production under long photoperiods \cite[p.~19]{tibbitts1994potato}. Yields from small plots can be inflated by light entering from the sides: individually caged potato plants, each on 0.2~m$^2$, yielded 28.5~g of tuber dry matter per square metre per day, against 21.9~g in a contiguous stand \cite[p.~82]{wheeler2006potato}.% SECONDARY: wheeler2006 reports Wheeler & Tibbitts 1987, 1988.
 The Wisconsin run was a contiguous stand, and in the wheat record the guard rows were excluded from the yield calculations, an exclusion the authors judged sufficient to remove the edge effect \cite[Fig.~1, p.~871; p.~873]{bugbee1988limits}. Asseng et al.\ label a figure of 700~t per hectare per year for wheat in a ten-layer indoor facility as measured, but it extrapolates one measured 70-day crop on a 1~m$^2$ edge-protected plot by an assumed five harvests per year and ten layers \cite[Abstract and p.~1; Fig.~2, p.~2; p.~3]{asseng2020wheat}.% SECONDARY: the 1 m2 crop is Monje & Bugbee 1998 (asseng ref. 13).
 Table~\ref{tab:nutrition-ce} lists the measured values with their light levels. Compared per square metre of growing area per year with the open-field yields of Table~\ref{tab:nutrition-field}, they are about 5 to 70 times higher, depending on the crop, the study and whether the world-average or the best national yield is used (our calculation). The comparison sets continuous cropping under high light and, in all three studies, elevated CO$_2$ against one field harvest per year.

The gain is bought with light. In Bugbee and Salisbury's wheat, yield kept rising up to the highest light level, while the efficiency with which the energy of photosynthetic photons was converted into biomass energy fell from over 10\% at low light to 7\% \cite[Abstract, p.~869; Fig.~2B, p.~873]{bugbee1988limits}. The electricity needed to supply the photons follows from the efficacy of the lamps. Among the independently tested fixtures compiled by Kusuma, Pattison and Bugbee, the most efficient reached about 3.0~$\mu$mol of photosynthetic photons per joule with blue and red LEDs and 2.8 with white and red LEDs, as of 2019 \cite[Table~2, p.~7]{kusuma2020led}.% SECONDARY: 3.0 and 2.78 are Design Lighting Consortium listings (kusuma ref. 58, 2019).
 Their calculated limits for fixtures built from the LEDs available in 2019, unprotected and with heat removal such as water cooling, are 3.4~$\mu$mol~J$^{-1}$ for white and red and 4.1 for blue and red, and protecting the LEDs from water and high humidity reduces these limits by about 10\% \cite[Abstract, p.~1; p.~6]{kusuma2020led}. The physical limits, at full conversion of electricity into photons, are 3.76~$\mu$mol~J$^{-1}$ for blue photons at 450~nm and 5.52 for red photons at 660~nm \cite[Fig.~2, p.~3]{kusuma2020led}. This section uses 3.0~$\mu$mol~J$^{-1}$, the best tested value, and 3.7, the blue and red limit after the loss for protection (our calculation). At these efficacies the lamps alone would use about 25 to 128~MWh per person per year across the datasets in Table~\ref{tab:nutrition-ce}, and 41 to 50~MWh for the NASA potato yield (our calculation). Lighting is not the whole electricity demand, but in simulated plant factories for lettuce, closed and artificially lit growing rooms (called vertical farms in other studies), its load exceeds all other loads at every location studied \cite[Section~4.1 and Fig.~4, p.~37; Fig.~5, p.~38]{graamans2018plant}.

Dividing the food energy of 2{,}400~kcal per day by the lamp electricity gives a food-to-electricity ratio of about 0.8 to 4.1\% (our calculation). This ratio counts lamp electricity only and food energy only, unlike Bugbee and Salisbury's, which relates total biomass energy to photon energy. Lovat, Noor and Milo, who call the same kind of ratio energetic efficiency and relate the food energy of the edible harvest to all purchased electricity, report a median of about 1\% across 19 vertical-farming studies, with a range of 0.4 to 1.9\%, and treat 1 to 2\% as a current best-practice rule of thumb; the studies were not harmonised, but all of them include lighting \cite[p.~2]{lovat2025vf}. Because their denominator is meant to cover all purchased electricity, although coverage beyond lighting varies between studies, the two ranges are not directly comparable.

Stacking reduces the floor area but not necessarily the total land. Van Delden et al.\ compute that nine square metres of lit growing layers, at 250~$\mu$mol~m$^{-2}$~s$^{-1}$ for 16~hours a day, need 28.2~m$^2$ of land for the photovoltaic panels that would power the lamps, at 52\textdegree{}N, with LEDs at a projected 3.5~$\mu$mol~J$^{-1}$ and with the energy of the climate system, such as cooling, excluded \cite[Fig.~4c and caption, p.~952]{vandelden2021vf}. Their comparison of the two light paths gives 316~MJ of photosynthetic radiation per square metre of land per year through panels and LEDs and 1{,}233~MJ through a glass greenhouse roof \cite[Fig.~4a--b, p.~952]{vandelden2021vf}. Applying van Delden's panel efficiency and land-area efficiency to the lamp energy of the NASA potato yield in Table~\ref{tab:nutrition-ce}, which comes from a larger growing area than the Wisconsin run, with photovoltaic yields from PVGIS \cite{jrc2026pvgis}, gives the land in Table~\ref{tab:nutrition-pv} (our calculation). At 3.7~$\mu$mol~J$^{-1}$ the panels alone need about 200~m$^2$ at three Costa Rican locations and 264~m$^2$ at 52\textdegree{}N, against 232~m$^2$ for potato at the best national field yield. Only lamp electricity is counted; the growing installation itself, its climate control and other loads, shading between panel rows and storage to match steady lamp demand to solar output are not included, so for this yield and these panel assumptions the figures understate the land needed. Kobayashi et al., counting all vertical-farm electricity with LEDs of 2.5~$\mu$mol~J$^{-1}$, find for Spain and Sweden that the land freed by growing wheat, potato or tomato indoors is not enough to produce that electricity with photovoltaics, and that lettuce passes only in their best case \cite[Abstract; Section~2.5, p.~3; Section~3.1.2 and Table~3, p.~4]{kobayashi2022vf}. With the improved technologies they assume for the future, including LEDs of 3.1~$\mu$mol~J$^{-1}$ and a photovoltaic efficiency of 50\%, which they give as the theoretical potential of multi-junction cells, the freed land suffices in the best case for every crop except tomato in Sweden \cite[Abstract; Section~2.5, p.~3; Table~4, p.~5; Section~3.2.3, p.~6]{kobayashi2022vf}.% SECONDARY: Kobayashi cites Bonsignore et al. 2020 for the 50% figure.

Electricity cost is a further constraint. Taking an energetic efficiency of 2\%, the upper end of their 1 to 2\% rule of thumb, chosen optimistically to allow for improvement, and an electricity cost of at least 4~cents per kWh, Lovat, Noor and Milo estimate an electricity cost of about \$10 per kilogram of dry plant matter, a lower bound that disregards all other costs, against producer prices generally below \$1 per kilogram, and as low as \$0.1, for dried cereals and pulses. They conclude that vertically farmed dried staples are unlikely to be economically viable in coming years as long as food prices, electricity costs and conversion efficiencies do not jointly change by one to two orders of magnitude \cite[pp.~2--3; Fig.~1, p.~3]{lovat2025vf}. Their analysis does not cover greenhouses \cite[p.~2]{lovat2025vf}.% SECONDARY: producer prices are FAOSTAT (Lovat's FAO 2024a); 4 c/kWh from levelised-cost sources cited by Lovat.
 Taken together, the measured research yields correspond to tens of square metres of growing area per person instead of hundreds of square metres of field, at a lamp electricity of about 25 to 128~MWh per person per year, and the land needed to generate that electricity depends on the site and the energy source.

\subsection{The greenhouse end of the range}\label{sec:nutrition-daylit}

Daylit greenhouses replace lamps with sunlight. Commercial yields are high for fruit vegetables: Dutch national averages for 2025, which the CBS table does not split between lit and unlit greenhouses, are 49.0~kg per square metre per year for tomato, 67.0 for cucumber and 26.6 for sweet pepper (our calculation from \cite{cbs2026groenteteelt}). These crops are low in food energy per kilogram, but at these yields tomato gives about 8{,}800~kcal per square metre per year and cucumber about 10{,}000, so that tomato alone would supply 2{,}400~kcal per day on about 100~m$^2$ (our calculation with USDA composition), at the energy cost of a Dutch greenhouse, which this section does not quantify per kilogram of tomato. The figure concerns energy only. Greenhouse yield is not a fixed quantity: Higashide and Heuvelink report, citing a Dutch reference handbook, that Dutch tomato yield doubled from 30~kg per square metre per year in the 1980s to 60 in 2005, and state that Japanese yields stayed at about 30 \cite[pp.~460--461]{higashide2009tomato}. The handbook figure is not the national average: CBS gives 47.3~kg for 2005 (our calculation from \cite{cbs2026groenteteelt}), about a fifth lower (our arithmetic).% SECONDARY: the handbook is KWIN Glastuinbouw 2005-2006 (PGB, Wageningen).

The energy cost of a greenhouse depends on the climate. In Graamans et al.'s simulations of lettuce production, given per kilogram of lettuce dry weight and so not directly comparable with the per-person figures above, the Dutch greenhouse uses 70~kWh of purchased energy per kilogram of dry weight, nearly all of it for heating, which the authors count one for one with electricity \cite[Abstract, p.~31; Section~2.4.2, p.~35; Fig.~5, p.~38]{graamans2018plant}. In Sweden the greenhouse needs supplementary lighting to produce year-round and then uses 182~kWh per kilogram; an unlit Swedish greenhouse, which the authors state is not viable without lighting, uses 211 \cite[Abstract, p.~31; Section~2.3.3, p.~34; Section~5, p.~39]{graamans2018plant}. Their hot-desert greenhouse needs no heating but uses 111~kWh of electricity per kilogram for cooling and dehumidification \cite[Abstract, p.~31; Fig.~5, p.~38]{graamans2018plant}, and a plant factory at the same site needs 158~kWh per kilogram more than that greenhouse \cite[Section~4.1, p.~37]{graamans2018plant}. The authors note that their crop model presumably underestimates dry-matter production at the high temperatures of the desert greenhouse and of the plant factories \cite[Section~4.3, p.~38]{graamans2018plant}. Evaporative cooling would be ineffective at the high ambient humidity of the desert site \cite[Section~2.3.3, p.~34]{graamans2018plant}, and greenhouses at the most extreme locations were not viable without features of plant factories such as lighting or active cooling \cite[Section~5, p.~39]{graamans2018plant}. None of the sources reviewed for this section measures the energy use of a greenhouse in the humid tropics. The heat limits of individual crops are listed in Section~\ref{sec:nutrition-crops}.

A unit can divide its growing area between daylit and lit production. With $A_{\mathrm{l}}$ the lit area, $L$ the daily light integral supplied by the lamps (mol~m$^{-2}$~d$^{-1}$) and $\eta$ the lamp efficacy ($\mu$mol~J$^{-1}$), the lamp electricity in kWh per year is
\begin{equation}
E_{\mathrm{lamp}} = \frac{365\,A_{\mathrm{l}}\,L}{3.6\,\eta},
\end{equation}
to which the energy for heating, cooling and dehumidification of the whole growing area must be added. In a fully lit area the lamp term does not depend on the climate; it depends on the site only where lamps supplement daylight. The climate-control term depends on the climate and is not yet quantified for tropical conditions. The operating point is a choice between more area and more energy. Table~\ref{tab:nutrition-ce} gives the lit end; for the greenhouse end this section gives areas for Dutch fruit vegetables but not their energy use.

\subsection{Energy source and emissions}\label{sec:nutrition-emissions}

The environmental case against long supply chains is set out in Section~\ref{sec:transport}. Two qualifications apply to the environmental case for local production. First, the share of emissions attributed to transport depends on the scope and the denominator. The 4.8\% in Section~\ref{sec:transport} is Crippa et al.'s estimate for 2015 (0.86~Gt CO$_2$e) as a share of a global food-system total of 18~Gt CO$_2$e that includes land use and land-use change, with transport counted as the food-system share of road, rail, shipping and aviation emissions \cite[pp.~198--200, 206]{crippa2021foodghg}. For United States household food in 1997, Weber and Matthews find that transport as a whole, including the transport of inputs to the farm and using a total that excludes land use, accounts for 11\% of life-cycle greenhouse gas emissions, and final delivery from producer to retail for 4\% \cite[Abstract, p.~3508; pp.~3509--3512]{weber2008foodmiles}. Li et al.\ estimate global food-miles emissions for 2017 at 1.4~Gt CO$_2$e for the transport of food itself and 3.0~Gt when the freight of upstream inputs such as fertiliser and machinery is included, 3.5 to 7.5 times earlier estimates, including Crippa et al.'s \cite[p.~446; p.~448]{li2022foodmiles}. The larger figure is about 19\% of a total made up of transport, food production and land-use change, and about 30\% without land-use change \cite[Abstract, p.~445; p.~446; Methods, p.~451]{li2022foodmiles}. Its production (7.1~Gt) and land-use-change (5.7~Gt) terms equal Crippa et al.'s farm-gate production and land-use-change stages \cite[p.~446]{li2022foodmiles}\cite[p.~199]{crippa2021foodghg}, so this total appears not to include the processing, packaging, retail, consumption and end-of-life emissions that Crippa et al.\ count (our comparison).% SECONDARY: the land-use-change term in the 19% denominator is taken from Crippa et al. 2021 (li ref. 22).
 In the same analysis, fruit and vegetables account for 36\% of food-miles emissions \cite[Abstract, p.~445]{li2022foodmiles}. Replacing all food imports with domestic production, a scenario the authors call hypothetical, would cut food-miles emissions by 0.27~Gt CO$_2$e, about 9\% of the 3.0~Gt (our arithmetic); the cut is small partly because local supply replaces shipping with road transport, which emits more per tonne-kilometre. The same scenario cuts production emissions by 0.11~Gt. Localising food only in high-income countries would instead cut 0.24~Gt from transport and 0.39~Gt from production, and the authors describe this reduction potential as appearing relatively limited \cite[pp.~448--449]{li2022foodmiles}.

Second, heated local production can emit more than distant production: tomatoes from a district-heated Austrian glasshouse emit 1.367~kg CO$_2$e per kilogram of packed product at a Viennese supermarket, of which 0.848~kg is from heating and 0.280~kg from CO$_2$ fertilisation, against 0.680 for tomatoes from unheated plastic greenhouses in Spain and 0.180 for unheated Austrian tunnels in season \cite[Table~2, p.~6]{theurl2014tomato}. With wood-chip heating, the authors estimate that the heated Austrian tomatoes would emit about half as much, similar to tomatoes imported from Italy or Almer\'ia \cite[pp.~8--9]{theurl2014tomato}.% SECONDARY: the 50% wood-chip estimate is from Theurl 2008 (same authors' report).

For an electrically lit unit, the emissions from the lamp energy computed above are set by the carbon intensity of its electricity. The global average in 2025 was 458~g CO$_2$e per kWh, and among the seven largest power sectors that Ember profiles the values range from 110 in Brazil to 670 in India \cite[pp.~23, 96, 109]{ember2026ger}; the 2025 values are estimates from monthly data and subject to revision \cite[pp.~113--114]{ember2026ger}. Costa Rica, where the project's first prototype will be tested, served 97.3\% of its demand from renewable sources in 2025 \cite[Section~3.2.1, p.~3]{ice2025renovable}. Its electricity utility reports a greenhouse gas emission factor for the national grid of 25.5 to 47.3~t per GWh in 2019--2022 (25.5 in 2020), 78.7 in 2023 and 114 in 2024 \cite[p.~58]{ice2024sostenibilidad}, a year it describes as one of the driest on record \cite[p.~5]{ice2024sostenibilidad}; the 2024 value was published before the 2024 emissions inventory \cite[p.~58]{ice2024sostenibilidad}. The lamp electricity for the NASA potato yield at 3.7~$\mu$mol~J$^{-1}$ (41~MWh per person per year) would therefore correspond to about 1.0~t CO$_2$e per year at Costa Rica's 2020 factor, 4.6~t at its 2024 factor, 19~t at the 2025 world average and 27~t at India's intensity (our calculation, lighting only). The two factors are not strictly comparable: ICE labels its factor t CO$_2$ on some pages and t CO$_2$e on others \cite[pp.~12, 25, 57--58]{ice2024sostenibilidad}, while Ember's intensities are CO$_2$-equivalent and include the life-cycle emissions of bioenergy \cite[p.~114]{ember2026ger}. Costa Rica is one data point; the project is intended for use worldwide, and the grid factor is an input to the per-user calculation, not a constant.

Roots and tubers supply energy. FAO's compilation of open-field data from developing countries gives conflicting rankings per hectare and day of growth. In its Table~9.3 potato and yam lead, maize ranks above sweet potato and cassava, and cassava below rice and wheat, while its Table~4.1, based like Table~9.3 on Horton et al.\ but with later data, ranks sweet potato above potato, rice and wheat \cite[p.~126; Table~9.3, p.~127; Table~4.1, p.~38]{fao1990roots}. Among the sources reviewed, ware potato, grown for eating rather than as seed, has been grown in controlled environments only in research (Section~\ref{sec:nutrition-ce} and Table~\ref{tab:nutrition-crops}). Potatoes exposed to bright light for long periods can build up glycoalkaloids, which normal cooking does not destroy; wounding and storage below 10~\textdegree{}C also raise them, and peeling reduces them \cite[pp.~100--101]{fao1990roots}.% SECONDARY: light, wounding and storage from Jadhav & Salunkhe 1975; peeling from Wood & Young 1974 and Bushway et al. 1983.
 Cassava can be stored dried: chips dried to 13\% moisture or less keep for several months if they are not exposed to humid air, and gari, a fermented cassava meal, keeps up to two years below 12\% moisture \cite[Section~5.1.1.5; Table~5.1]{fao1998rootsstorage}, although the same report gives the moisture of traditionally fried gari as about 18\% in one place and 8\% in another \cite[Section~5.1.2.4; Table~5.2]{fao1998rootsstorage}. The final drying of chips needs air at no more than 65\% relative humidity; in rainy seasons, when humidity often exceeds 80\%, drying can take several days and contamination, including aflatoxin, becomes a major problem; the report describes suspended drying trays, solar dryers and wood-fired dryers as ways to shorten drying \cite[Section~5.1.1.4]{fao1998rootsstorage}.% SECONDARY: Table 5.1 is adapted from Muchnik & Vinck 1984; the 18% gari moisture cites Muchnik & Vivier 1984 and Bencini 1991.

Legumes and oilseeds supply protein and fat. Soybean and peanut have been grown to seed in research chambers: the NASA chamber's best soybean yield was 6.0~g of edible dry matter per square metre per day \cite[p.~708; Table~2, p.~711]{wheeler2008crop}, and peanut grown in nutrient film in small growth chambers gave 275 and 343~g of seed per square metre 110 days after planting, at photosynthetic photon fluxes of 350 and 700~$\mu$mol~m$^{-2}$~s$^{-1}$ for 12~hours a day, averaged over CO$_2$ levels of 400 to 1{,}600~ppm \cite[p.~328; Table~2, p.~333]{mortley2001peanut}; the moisture basis of these seed weights is not stated. Speed-breeding studies of soybean and peanut shorten generation times and report at most seed or pod numbers per plant, not yield per area \cite[Abstract; p.~6]{jahne2020speed}\cite[Abstract, p.~107; Table~1, p.~110]{oconnor2013peanut}, and Watson et al.\ note that, in a breeding context, phenotypes such as yield measured in controlled conditions do not always correlate with field observations \cite[p.~26]{watson2018speed}. Storage is conditional. Peanuts (groundnuts) can be stored for a year at 25--27~\textdegree{}C if shelled kernels are dried to no more than 7\% moisture (9\% unshelled) and the air is kept at 70\% relative humidity, and the same review cites 7.5\% moisture, 10~\textdegree{}C and 65\% relative humidity as optimal bulk storage conditions \cite[Section~4]{waliyar2015aflatoxin}.% SECONDARY: waliyar's 7%/9%/70% RH/25-27 C from Odogola 1994 and Waliyar et al. 2007, 2008; 7.5%/10 C/65% RH from Pattee & Young 1982.


Fruits, vegetables, roots and tubers are lost after harvest at high rates. In FAO's food loss index for 2016, they lose about 22\% from after harvest up to, but not including, retail, against about 9\% for cereals and pulses; these values are read from a chart, partly model-based and weighted by economic value, and the same report attributes the highest group loss, about 25\% for roots, tubers and oil-bearing crops, mainly to cassava and potato, for which many data were reported \cite[Fig.~4, p.~9]{fao2019sofa}.

\textit{Agaricus bisporus} does not require light for fruiting \cite[Abstract, p.~1]{baars2020agaricus}. Its cultivation is energy-intensive because temperature, humidity and ventilation are controlled, and its energy demand and impact depend on the climate and on the electricity grid at the production site \cite[Section~4.1, p.~8]{goglio2024agaricus}; on average across the systems and impact categories assessed, composting is the largest contributor to its environmental impact, followed by electricity and casing \cite[Abstract]{goglio2024agaricus}. A life-cycle study of United States production gives 29.1~MJ of primary energy per kilogram of fresh mushrooms at the facility gate, of which electricity is 37\% and fuels 38\%, with diesel used mostly for trucks and machinery that mix and move compost and heating oil used mainly in colder regions \cite[Sections~2.1, 3.2 and 4.1.1]{robinson2019agaricus}. At the dry-matter and protein contents assumed by Goglio et al., freshly harvested mushrooms contain about 2.5\% protein when grown for the fresh market and 3.4\% when grown for drying (our arithmetic from \cite[Section~4.2, p.~9]{goglio2024agaricus}). In experiments summarised by Baars et al., \textit{Agaricus} primordia kept at 25~\textdegree{}C did not develop beyond the smooth, undifferentiated stage, while lowering the temperature to 18~\textdegree{}C, together with ventilation to reduce CO$_2$, allowed fruiting to proceed \cite[Section~4, p.~10]{baars2020agaricus}, which suggests that cooling would be needed in the lowland tropics. Five oyster mushroom (\textit{Pleurotus}) species have reported optimal fruiting ranges reaching 28 to 30~\textdegree{}C, while \textit{P.~ostreatus}, \textit{P.~pulmonarius} and \textit{P.~eryngii} are given 20 to 25~\textdegree{}C. Four species, two of them among those five (\textit{P.~abalonus} and \textit{P.~cornucopiae}), are given induction temperatures of 10 to 18~\textdegree{}C \cite[Table~1, p.~640]{bellettini2019pleurotus}. The same review notes that high temperatures can reduce mushroom development and favour competing microorganisms \cite[Section~3.1.2, p.~640]{bellettini2019pleurotus}, and that \textit{Pleurotus} grows on agricultural residues \cite[Section~2.1, p.~635]{bellettini2019pleurotus}.% SECONDARY: Baars summarises Eastwood et al. 2013; Bellettini Table 1 compiles Stamets 2000, Oei & Nieuwenhuijzen 2005 and others, and the high-temperature statement cites Urben 2004; Goglio's climate-control requirements cite Carrasco et al. 2021 and its protein value Dimopoulou et al. 2022; the climate and grid dependence is Goglio citing Robinson et al. 2019.

The evidence therefore falls into three groups. Fruit vegetables, leafy greens and mushrooms are produced commercially in controlled environments \cite{cbs2026groenteteelt}\cite[Sections~2.1--2.2, pp.~2--3]{goglio2024agaricus}, and pre-basic seed potato minitubers in greenhouses and aeroponic systems \cite[p.~5]{cip2010aeroponics}. Ware potato, wheat, soybean and peanut have been grown to harvest in research facilities. For sweet potato, cassava and yam, no controlled-environment yield data were found among the sources reviewed.

\subsection{Nutrients, not only calories}\label{sec:nutrition-nutrients}

Energy is one of many requirements. This section uses the FAO/WHO recommended nutrient intakes for vitamins and minerals, which for vitamin A are given as recommended safe intakes and which for vitamin D, vitamin B12 and iodine are adopted from other expert bodies \cite[p.~xvi; pp.~35, 52, 284, 311]{faowho2004vitmin}. For protein it uses the EFSA population reference intake of 0.83~g per kilogram of body weight per day, which is derived for protein with a protein digestibility-corrected amino acid score (PD-CAAS) of 1.0 and applies to usual mixed diets in Europe \cite[Section~3.4, p.~19; Table~3, p.~24]{efsa2017drv}.% SECONDARY: the value was set in EFSA's 2012 protein opinion (EFSA Journal 10(2):2557, doi:10.2903/j.efsa.2012.2557), not held.
 Several requirements cannot be met by choosing crops. No plant has the enzymes to synthesise vitamin B12 \cite[Section~14.1, p.~279]{faowho2004vitmin}. In most locations between latitudes 42\textdegree{}N and 42\textdegree{}S, synthesis in the skin is the most physiologically relevant way to acquire vitamin D, and at higher latitudes in winter this synthesis is virtually zero \cite[pp.~53--54]{faowho2004vitmin}. The iodine content of food depends on the iodine content of the soil in which it grows, and the selenium content on environmental conditions and agricultural practice, including selenium added to fertilisers \cite[p.~305; pp.~200--201]{faowho2004vitmin}. Other requirements depend on assumptions about absorption and conversion. The requirement for iron depends on how much the diet allows to be absorbed: for menstruating women it is 29.4~mg per day at 10\% bioavailability and 58.8~mg at 5\% \cite[Table~13.5, p.~271]{faowho2004vitmin}. For vitamin A the report gives conflicting conversion factors for carotenes, recommending 1:14 for mixed vegetable diets in one place and retaining 1:6 in another \cite[pp.~26, 29]{faowho2004vitmin}.

The minimum area that meets all requirements at once can be found by linear programming: choose the area of each crop so that the total area is smallest while every nutrient constraint is met. Masuda, Tako and Nitta applied this method to the Japanese Closed Ecology Experiment Facilities. With 18 nutrients, Japanese recommended allowances for sedentary men, light levels of 46.3 and 28.3~mol~m$^{-2}$~d$^{-1}$, vitamins B12 and D supplied as tablets, and each of 21 supplementary crops held between 0.125 and 1.5~m$^2$ for variety, they obtained 129.14~m$^2$ for two people, which they report as 64.8~m$^2$ per person, or 74.5~m$^2$ with 15\% added for seedlings \cite[pp.~62--64]{masuda2005ceef}. DeFries et al.\ propose a related metric for a single food item, the number of adults whose requirement for a nutrient one hectare of it can meet in a year \cite[p.~240]{defries2015metrics}.

We applied the linear-programming method to open-field yields (our calculation). With world-average FAOSTAT yields, USDA composition, FAO/WHO intakes for adults aged 19 to 50, iron at 10\% bioavailability and vitamin A at 1:6, the minimum area is 443, 494 and 562~m$^2$ per person at 2{,}100, 2{,}400 and 2{,}800~kcal per day, the same for women and men. The binding constraints are energy, pantothenate and calcium, and the solution uses maize, carrots and spinach. Protein is not binding at the EFSA reference value: in the base cases supply is 1.19 to 1.82 times the requirement (our calculation). That reference value assumes protein with a PD-CAAS of 1.0, so the margin could shrink once protein quality is taken into account; the protein quality of this all-plant mix was not assessed, and the indispensable amino acid content or digestibility of plant proteins is usually lower than that of animal proteins \cite[Section~3.4, p.~18]{efsa2017drv}. At 2{,}400~kcal per day, iron at 5\% bioavailability raises the area for women to 543~m$^2$; best national yields lower it to 237~m$^2$; limiting each crop to a quarter of energy intake gives 538 to 542~m$^2$. Including all FAO/WHO upper limits makes the problem infeasible because of the magnesium limit of 350~mg per day. The report takes this limit from the US Food and Nutrition Board and gives it for intake ``from foods and drinking water'', while in the same section it describes dietary magnesium as relatively innocuous and reports harm only from magnesium salts \cite[p.~225]{faowho2004vitmin}. Whether the limit is meant to cover magnesium in ordinary food has to be checked in the original before the infeasibility is treated as a real constraint; without that limit the result is unchanged. Vitamin B12, vitamin D, iodine and selenium are not constrained, for the reasons given above, nor is biotin, for lack of composition data; the protein requirement uses reference body weights of 58.5~kg for women and 68.1~kg for men \cite[Table~17, p.~91]{efsa2017drv}. Applied to the five crops measured in the NASA chamber \cite[Table~2, p.~711]{wheeler2008crop}, the same method gives 41.6 to 51.5~m$^2$ of growing area per person, mostly potato with lettuce and tomato, with energy, vitamin~E and calcium binding and lamps using 39 to 52~MWh per year at 3.7~$\mu$mol~J$^{-1}$ (our calculation). These are minimum areas, not diets: they exclude losses, seed, rotation, paths, cooking losses and acceptability, and unlike Masuda's they impose no minimum variety.

An urban garden plot shows how energy sets most of the area. Baldi, Gallo and Lenzi estimated the production of 50~m$^2$ social-garden plots in Prato, Italy, of which about 30~m$^2$ were cultivated and 81\% of that with tomato; the yields were not measured but computed from Italian average yields \cite[pp.~2--3, 5--6; Fig.~4, p.~7]{baldi2024garden}. Against Italian reference values for people over 75, they report that one plot covers 59\% (men) to 73\% (women) of one person's vitamin~C, 20\% (men) to 24\% (women) of vitamin~A and 2.9\% of calcium \cite[Table~4, p.~9]{baldi2024garden}. Recomputing the same plot against FAO/WHO intakes for adults with USDA composition data (our calculation), it covers about 97\% of vitamin~C, 41 to 50\% of vitamin~A, 3\% of calcium, 4 to 5\% of protein and 2\% of energy. A vegetable plot of this size can supply a large part of some vitamins and almost none of the energy, and the energy requirement sets most of the hundreds of square metres in the open-field result: maize alone would meet the energy requirement on 424~m$^2$, and the other binding constraints raise the minimum to 494~m$^2$ (our calculation).

\subsection{From bounds to a per-user answer}\label{sec:nutrition-peruser}

The quantity the project needs for each user is the same linear program with site-specific coefficients: for each candidate crop, its yield and its energy cost at that site, daylit or lit, and the user's own requirements. The objective can be the smallest energy use, or a weighted combination of energy and area, subject to the nutrient constraints. Because area and energy pull against each other, each weighting gives one point of a trade-off set rather than a single optimum. The proposed solution (Section~\ref{sec:solution}) describes the system intended to compute it.% TODO: solution.tex predates this analysis and is to be revised (status note there).

The coefficients have to be estimated with crop models, and the evidence on how well such models transfer between sites is mixed. A greenhouse tomato model whose parameters were taken from the literature and not calibrated simulated dry-matter yield very well in Dutch greenhouses, accurately in a high-technology Spanish greenhouse and with fair accuracy in a low-technology one, which it underestimated by about 10\% (our arithmetic). It overestimated growth-chamber yields by 33\% on average; the initial temperature sum was fitted to the data for the Spanish and growth-chamber runs \cite[Section~4, p.~387; Sections~5.1--5.3, pp.~390--391]{vanthoor2011tomato}. Its authors suggest calibrating cardinal temperatures for each cultivar and note that performance can be improved by calibrating the temperature-inhibition and photosynthesis functions and the fruit growth period \cite[Section~5.2, p.~390; Section~5.5, p.~393]{vanthoor2011tomato}. In a comparison of 27 wheat models, simulated yields varied widely when the modellers had no access to observed yields and growth data, although the medians were close to the observations; after calibration against those same data more than half of the simulated yields fell within the mean variation of field experiments \cite[p.~827; Fig.~1, p.~828]{asseng2013uncertainty}. The spread among the models' simulated responses to warming grew with temperature \cite[p.~829]{asseng2013uncertainty}; for a high-emission late-century scenario, full calibration lowered the coefficient of variation of the partially calibrated models' simulated responses, 20 to 30\%, by what the authors report as 2 to 7\%, and selecting the models that performed best under present conditions did not reduce it \cite[pp.~827, 829]{asseng2013uncertainty}; none of the 27 models had been tested with elevated CO$_2$ and high temperature together \cite[p.~829]{asseng2013uncertainty}. In two calibration exercises involving 29 modelling groups, Wallach et al.\ found no consensus on how to calibrate even the phenology component of crop models, including among groups using the same model structure on the same data, and stressed that default parameter values, which most parameters keep, should reflect what is known about the cultivars and environments of interest \cite[Section~2, p.~3; Section~4, p.~7; Section~4.2, p.~8]{wallach2021chaos}.

On this evidence, a model with literature parameters can transfer to some conditions, as the tomato model did to a high-technology Spanish greenhouse with only its initial temperature sum fitted, and less well to others: in growth chambers at 1{,}000~ppm CO$_2$ it overestimated yield by a third, which its authors attribute to conditions unlike those of the greenhouses for which it was developed \cite[Sections~4.3 and 5.2--5.3, pp.~389--391]{vanthoor2011tomato}. Wallach et al.'s point that default values should reflect the cultivars and environments of interest \cite[Section~4.2, p.~8]{wallach2021chaos} implies that defaults chosen for one setting need not suit another (our inference). For the phenology component, and in their view probably more widely, choosing which parameters to calibrate requires knowledge of the model, the environments and the reliability of the data, so that a fully automatic calibration procedure may not be advisable \cite[Section~4.4, p.~9]{wallach2021chaos}. The measurements of each unit would supply the data for calibration in its own conditions. A per-user result is therefore an estimate with an uncertainty. Local calibration narrows it for the conditions that were measured. Outside them, and in particular for the combination of elevated CO$_2$ and high temperature that a CO$_2$-enriched unit in the tropics would face, the evidence above gives no assurance: none of the wheat models compared had been tested under it, and the tomato model degraded in growth chambers. Asseng et al.\ conclude that individual models fail to represent the uncertainty in crop responses to climate change, which model ensembles have the potential to quantify \cite[p.~830]{asseng2013uncertainty}; we expect the same to hold for a per-user estimate (our inference).
