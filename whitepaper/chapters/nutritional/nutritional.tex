\section{Food self-sufficiency as a computable quantity}\label{sec:nutrition-analysis}

Whether a food-production system can make its user food-independent cannot be stated in advance for every user. It depends on the crops grown, the growing area and daylight available, the local climate and the energy the user can supply, so it has to be computed for each configuration. The requirement side varies in the same way. Human energy requirements are calculated as the basal metabolic rate multiplied by a physical activity level (PAL), and they differ with sex, age, body weight and habitual activity. The FAO/WHO/UNU expert consultation states that its tabulated requirements apply to population groups, and that the requirement of a specific individual must be based on that person's own energy expenditure or on estimates that take their habitual activity into account \cite[Section~5.4.1, p.~40]{faowhounu2004energy}. Its tables range, for example, from about 2{,}100~kcal per day for women aged 18 to 30 weighing 55~kg at a PAL of 1.60 \cite[Table~5.7]{faowhounu2004energy} to about 2{,}800~kcal per day (11.7~MJ) for men of the same age, 1.69~m tall with a body mass index of 21 and a PAL of 1.75 \cite[Section~5.4.2, p.~40]{faowhounu2004energy}. The EAT--Lancet Commission uses 2{,}400~kcal per day as the average global energy intake of adults \cite[p.~1633]{rockstrom2025eatlancet}. The calculations in this section use 2{,}400~kcal per day and are repeated at 2{,}100 and 2{,}800.

This section estimates the range within which the per-configuration computation falls. It combines open-field yield statistics, published measurements of crops grown in controlled environments and published nutrient requirements, and it reports area and energy per person for each stated configuration rather than a single verdict. The estimates are for orientation only. Each one rests on yields measured elsewhere, under conditions that differ from any particular installation, and they are to be replaced by measurements from the project's own prototypes as these become available. Figures labelled ``our calculation'' are reproduced by the scripts in the project repository (\texttt{whitepaper/food\_data\_analysis/snut\_analysis}).

\subsection{The open-field baseline}\label{sec:nutrition-field}

The theoretical ceiling on photosynthesis is the starting point. Zhu, Long and Ort calculate that the maximum efficiency with which solar energy can be converted into biomass is 4.6\% for C3 photosynthesis and 6\% for C4 photosynthesis, at 30~\textdegree{}C and an atmospheric CO$_2$ concentration of 380~ppm, and that the C4 advantage disappears as CO$_2$ nears 700~ppm \cite[Abstract, p.~153]{zhu2008maximum}. The figures refer to total incident solar energy and total biomass, not to the harvested product. The C3 ceiling also falls as temperature rises \cite[Fig.~3, p.~156]{zhu2008maximum}, which matters for a tropical site. Observed maximum efficiencies are, with few exceptions, about one third of the theoretical value \cite[p.~243]{zhu2010photosynthesis}.% SECONDARY: zhu2010 attributes this to its ref. 13.
 Part of the gap lies in the growing conditions: crops do not use the whole period in which temperature and water would allow growth \cite[p.~239]{zhu2010photosynthesis}, and nitrogen fertiliser and irrigation raise the efficiency of major crops \cite[p.~243]{zhu2010photosynthesis}. Season length, water, nutrients and CO$_2$ are conditions that a controlled environment can change.

What fields deliver in practice can be computed from production statistics. Combining FAOSTAT yields for 2022 \cite{faostat2025qcl} with USDA food composition data \cite{usdafdc2018srlegacy} (our calculation), world-average open-field yields give about 2{,}070~kcal per square metre per year for maize and 1{,}720 for potato, assuming one harvest per year. The highest national averages are about 4{,}170 (maize, Spain) and 3{,}780 (potato, United States). Table~\ref{tab:nutrition-field} converts these into the area needed to supply one person's energy. Energy-dense seeds do not change the picture: groundnut kernels carry about 5{,}630~kcal per kilogram but yield about 710~kcal per square metre per year at the world-average yield (our calculation), because their yield per unit area is low. The one-harvest assumption overstates the area for short-cycle crops in climates that allow several crops per year.

\subsection{Buying area with light}\label{sec:nutrition-ce}

Controlled-environment research gives much higher yields per square metre of growing area, at a high input of light. In NASA's Biomass Production Chamber, crops were grown on 20~m$^2$ of growing area spread over four stacked shelves, under high-pressure sodium or metal halide lamps and 1{,}000 to 1{,}200~ppm CO$_2$ \cite[Section~2.1, p.~707]{wheeler2008crop}. The highest edible productivity was 18.4~g of potato dry matter per square metre per day at a daily light integral of 42.2~mol~m$^{-2}$~d$^{-1}$ \cite[Table~2, p.~711]{wheeler2008crop}, which corresponds to about 68~kcal per square metre per day (our calculation). In a run at the University of Wisconsin with a transplant scheme and continuous light for most of the cycle, potato reached 37.5~g of tuber dry matter per square metre per day at 52.4~mol~m$^{-2}$~d$^{-1}$, from which Wheeler computes 141~kcal per square metre per day and 17.8~m$^2$ of potato to supply 2{,}500~kcal per day for one person \cite[Table~1, p.~83; p.~84]{wheeler2006potato}. Tibbitts, Cao and Wheeler report the same productivity (139~kcal per square metre per day) and an area of 20.1~m$^2$ for 2{,}800~kcal per day, assuming that the whole harvest is used and that nothing is lost in storage; they propose 24~m$^2$ to allow for losses \cite[p.~30]{tibbitts1994potato}. The best wheat yield reviewed here is 60~g of grain per square metre per day under 150~mol~m$^{-2}$~d$^{-1}$ and 1{,}200~ppm CO$_2$ \cite[Abstract, p.~869]{bugbee1988limits}.

These are research results with conditions attached. The best potato run is a single stand of 16 harvested plants in containers of peat and vermiculite, not a hydroponic system: in the same report, consistent tuber production was obtained only when solid media were used \cite[p.~6; Table~3.4, p.~29]{tibbitts1994potato}. Yields from small plots can be inflated by light entering from the sides \cite[p.~220]{wheeler1996bpc}\cite[p.~82]{wheeler2006potato}, and the wheat record comes from plots of 0.2~m$^2$ \cite[p.~873]{bugbee1988limits}. A widely quoted indoor wheat figure of 700~t per hectare is not a measurement: it scales one 1~m$^2$ crop by an assumed five harvests per year and ten layers \cite[p.~1]{asseng2020wheat}. Table~\ref{tab:nutrition-ce} lists the measured values with their light levels. Compared per square metre of growing area per year with the open-field yields of Table~\ref{tab:nutrition-field}, they are about 5 to 70 times higher, depending on the crop, the study and whether the world-average or the best national yield is used (our calculation).

The gain is bought with light. Productivity in these chambers is closely tied to light intensity, while the efficiency with which light is converted into biomass tends to fall as intensity rises \cite[p.~711]{wheeler2008crop}; in Bugbee and Salisbury's wheat the conversion efficiency fell from over 10\% at low light to 7\% at the highest light level \cite[Abstract, p.~869]{bugbee1988limits}. The electricity needed to supply the photons follows from the efficacy of the lamps. The best LED fixtures tested by Kusuma, Pattison and Bugbee deliver up to 3.0~$\mu$mol of photosynthetic photons per joule \cite[Table~2, p.~7]{kusuma2020led}, fixtures built from current technology could reach 3.4 to 4.1 \cite[Fig.~6, p.~7]{kusuma2020led}, and the physical limits are 3.76~$\mu$mol~J$^{-1}$ for blue and 5.52 for red light \cite[Fig.~2, p.~3]{kusuma2020led}. Protecting the LEDs from water and high humidity reduces these values by about 10\% \cite[p.~1]{kusuma2020led}. At 3.0 to 4.1~$\mu$mol~J$^{-1}$, the lamps alone would use about 22 to 128~MWh per person per year across the datasets in Table~\ref{tab:nutrition-ce}, and 37 to 50~MWh for the NASA potato yield (our calculation). Lighting is not the whole electricity demand, but in simulated plant factories its load exceeds all other loads at every location studied \cite[Section~4.1, p.~37]{graamans2018plant}. Dividing the food energy of 2{,}400~kcal per day by the lamp electricity gives conversion efficiencies of about 0.8 to 4.6\% (our calculation, lighting only). Lovat, Noor and Milo report a median of about 1\% across 19 vertical-farming studies, with a range of 0.4 to 1.9\%, and they treat 1 to 2\% as a current best-practice rule of thumb \cite[p.~2]{lovat2025vf}.

Stacking reduces the floor area but not necessarily the total land. Van Delden et al.\ compute that nine square metres of lit growing layers need 28.2~m$^2$ of land for the photovoltaic panels that would power the lamps, at 52\textdegree{}N, with LEDs of 3.5~$\mu$mol~J$^{-1}$ and with cooling excluded \cite[Fig.~4c, p.~952]{vandelden2021vf}. Their comparison of the two light paths gives 316~MJ of photosynthetic radiation per square metre of land per year through panels and LEDs and 1{,}233~MJ through a glass greenhouse roof \cite[Fig.~4a--b, p.~952]{vandelden2021vf}. Applying van Delden's panel efficiency and land use to the potato lamp energy of Table~\ref{tab:nutrition-ce}, with photovoltaic yields from PVGIS \cite{jrc2026pvgis}, gives the land in Table~\ref{tab:nutrition-pv} (our calculation). At 52\textdegree{}N the panels need about as much land as the best national potato field; at three Costa Rican locations they need somewhat less. Kobayashi et al.\ reach a similar result for Spain and Sweden: with current technology, the land freed by growing wheat, potato or tomato indoors is not enough to produce their electricity with photovoltaics, and only lettuce passes this test \cite[Abstract; Table~3, p.~4]{kobayashi2022vf}.

The economics point the same way. At an energetic efficiency of 2\% and an electricity cost of at least 4~cents per kWh, Lovat, Noor and Milo estimate an electricity cost of about \$10 per kilogram of dry plant matter, against producer prices below \$1 per kilogram, and as low as \$0.1, for dried cereals and pulses. They conclude that vertically farmed dried staples are unlikely to be economically viable as long as food prices, electricity costs and conversion efficiencies do not jointly change by one to two orders of magnitude \cite[pp.~2--3]{lovat2025vf}. The result of this subsection is therefore not a single bound but a trade-off: area per person can be reduced from hundreds of square metres to tens, at a lamp electricity of tens of megawatt-hours per person per year, and the land needed to generate that electricity depends on the site and the energy source.

\subsection{The daylit end of the range}\label{sec:nutrition-daylit}

Daylit greenhouses replace lamps with sunlight. Commercial yields are high for fruit vegetables: Dutch national averages for 2025 are 49.0~kg per square metre per year for tomato, 67.0 for cucumber and 26.6 for sweet pepper (our calculation from \cite{cbs2026groenteteelt}). These averages mix lit and unlit production, and they are fresh weights of crops low in food energy. Greenhouse yield is not a fixed quantity: Dutch tomato yield is reported to have doubled from 30~kg per square metre per year in the 1980s to 60 in 2005, while Japanese yields stayed at about 30 \cite[pp.~460--461]{higashide2009tomato}. The 60~kg figure comes from a Dutch reference handbook that Higashide and Heuvelink cite; the national average from CBS for 2005 is 47.3~kg (our calculation from \cite{cbs2026groenteteelt}).

The energy cost of a greenhouse depends on the climate. In Graamans et al.'s simulations of lettuce production, the Dutch greenhouse uses 70~kWh of purchased energy per kilogram of dry weight and the Swedish one 211, nearly all of it heat, which the authors count one for one with electricity \cite[Abstract, p.~31; Section~2.4.2, p.~35]{graamans2018plant}. Their hot-desert greenhouse needs no heating but uses 111~kWh of electricity per kilogram for cooling and dehumidification, against 247 for the plant factory \cite[Abstract, p.~31]{graamans2018plant}. Evaporative cooling would be ineffective at high ambient humidity \cite[Section~2.3.3, p.~34]{graamans2018plant}, and greenhouses at the most extreme locations were not viable without features of plant factories such as lighting or active cooling \cite[Section~5, p.~39]{graamans2018plant}. None of the sources reviewed for this section measures the energy use of a greenhouse in the humid tropics. The heat limits of individual crops are listed in Section~\ref{sec:nutrition-crops}.

A unit can divide its growing area between daylit and lit production. With $A_{\mathrm{d}}$ the daylit area, $A_{\mathrm{l}}$ the lit area, $L$ the daily light integral supplied by the lamps (mol~m$^{-2}$~d$^{-1}$) and $\eta$ the lamp efficacy ($\mu$mol~J$^{-1}$), the lamp electricity in kWh per year is
\begin{equation}
E_{\mathrm{lamp}} = \frac{365\,A_{\mathrm{l}}\,L}{3.6\,\eta},
\end{equation}
to which the energy for heating, cooling and dehumidification of both areas must be added. Both terms depend on the site, and the second is not yet quantified for tropical conditions. The operating point is a choice between more area and more energy, and the tables above give its two ends.

\subsection{Energy source and emissions}\label{sec:nutrition-emissions}

The environmental case against long supply chains is set out in Section~\ref{sec:transport}. Two qualifications apply to any local production that uses energy. First, the share of food-system emissions attributed to transport depends on what is counted: 4.8\% for the transport of food itself (Section~\ref{sec:transport}), 4\% for final delivery and 11\% for the whole supply chain in United States household food \cite[Abstract, p.~3508]{weber2008foodmiles}, and about 19\% when the freight of all upstream inputs such as fertiliser and machinery is included \cite[Abstract, p.~445]{li2022foodmiles}. In the same global analysis, fruit and vegetables account for 36\% of food-miles emissions \cite[Abstract, p.~445]{li2022foodmiles}, while replacing all imports with domestic production would cut food-miles emissions by only 0.27~Gt CO$_2$e, because road transport emits more per tonne-kilometre than shipping \cite[p.~448]{li2022foodmiles}. Second, heated local production can emit more than distant production: tomatoes from a district-heated Austrian glasshouse emit 1.367~kg CO$_2$e per kilogram at a Viennese supermarket, against 0.680 for tomatoes from unheated plastic greenhouses in Spain and 0.180 for unheated Austrian tunnels in season \cite[Table~2]{theurl2014tomato}.

For an electrically lit unit the decisive input is the carbon intensity of its electricity. The global average in 2025 was 458~g CO$_2$e per kWh, with 110 in Brazil and 670 in India among the countries Ember reports \cite[pp.~23, 96, 109]{ember2026ger}. Costa Rica, where the project's first prototype will be tested, served 97.3\% of its demand from renewable sources in 2025 \cite[Section~3.2.1, p.~3]{ice2025renovable}, but its grid emission factor varied from 25.5 to 47.3~t CO$_2$ per GWh in 2019--2022 to 78.7 in 2023 and 114 in 2024 \cite[p.~58]{ice2024sostenibilidad}, a year the utility describes as one of the driest on record \cite[p.~5]{ice2024sostenibilidad}. The lamp electricity for the NASA potato yield at 4.1~$\mu$mol~J$^{-1}$ (37~MWh per person per year) would therefore correspond to about 0.9~t CO$_2$e per year at Costa Rica's 2020 factor, 4.2~t at its 2024 factor, 17~t at the 2025 world average and 25~t at India's intensity (our calculation, lighting only). Costa Rica is one data point; the project is intended for use worldwide, and the grid factor is an input to the per-user calculation, not a constant.

Roots and tubers supply energy. In open-field data from developing countries, roots generally give more edible energy per hectare and day of growth than cereals, but maize ranks above sweet potato and cassava, and cassava below rice and wheat \cite[p.~126; Table~9.3, p.~127]{fao1990roots}. Ware potato has been grown as food in controlled environments only in research (Section~\ref{sec:nutrition-ce}). The commercial use of aeroponic potato production is the multiplication of seed minitubers \cite[p.~5]{cip2010aeroponics}, and tubers exposed to light for long periods can build up glycoalkaloids that cooking does not destroy \cite[p.~101]{fao1990roots}. Tubers can be stored as dried chips and flour: chips dried to 13\% moisture keep for several months if they are protected from humid air, and gari keeps up to two years below 12\% moisture \cite[Section~5.1.1.5; Table~5.1]{fao1998rootsstorage}. Drying is the constraint in humid climates. The final drying of chips needs air at no more than 65\% relative humidity, and in rainy seasons, when humidity often exceeds 80\%, drying takes several days and contamination, including aflatoxin, becomes a major problem \cite[Section~5.1.1.4]{fao1998rootsstorage}.

Legumes and oilseeds supply protein and fat. Soybean and peanut have been grown to seed in research chambers: the NASA chamber's best soybean yield was 6.0~g of edible dry matter per square metre per day \cite[Table~2, p.~711]{wheeler2008crop}, and peanut grown in nutrient film for 110 days gave 275 to 343~g of seed per square metre at two light levels \cite[Table~2, p.~333]{mortley2001peanut}. Speed-breeding studies of soybean, peanut and canola shorten generation times; they do not measure food yield, and their authors note that yield in controlled conditions does not always correlate with field observations \cite[p.~26]{watson2018speed}\cite{jahne2020speed,oconnor2013peanut}. Storage is conditional. Groundnuts can be stored for a year at 25--27~\textdegree{}C if shelled kernels are dried to 7\% moisture (9\% unshelled) and the air is kept at 70\% relative humidity \cite[p.~4]{waliyar2015aflatoxin}, and extracted oils oxidise faster when warm, exposed to light and oxygen, and more unsaturated, as soybean and sunflower oils are \cite[pp.~175--176]{choe2006oil}.

Vegetables and fruits supply most micronutrients and have the highest commercial yields per square metre under glass. Their water footprint per kilogram is low but per kilocalorie it is high: 322~m$^3$ per tonne and 1.34~litres per kcal for vegetables, against 387~m$^3$ per tonne and 0.47~litres per kcal for roots and tubers \cite[Table~2, p.~1584]{mekonnen2011crops}. They are also lost after harvest at high rates. In FAO's food loss index for 2016, fruits and vegetables lose about 21\% between harvest and retail, against about 9\% for cereals and pulses; these values are read from a chart, and the same report attributes the highest group loss, about 25\% for roots, tubers and oil-bearing crops, mainly to cassava and potato \cite[Fig.~4, p.~9]{fao2019sofa}.

Mushrooms need no light in the case of \textit{Agaricus bisporus} \cite[Abstract, p.~1]{baars2020agaricus}, but their cultivation is energy-intensive because temperature, humidity and ventilation must be controlled \cite[Section~4.1, p.~8]{goglio2024agaricus}; a life-cycle study of United States production gives 29.1~MJ of primary energy per kilogram \cite[Section~3.2]{robinson2019agaricus}. At the dry-matter and protein contents assumed by Goglio et al., mushrooms contain about 2.5\% protein by fresh weight (our arithmetic from \cite[Section~4.2, p.~9]{goglio2024agaricus}). \textit{Agaricus} stops developing when the growing room is held at 25~\textdegree{}C and fruits when it is lowered to 18~\textdegree{}C \cite[Section~4]{baars2020agaricus}, which implies cooling in lowland tropics. Several oyster mushroom (\textit{Pleurotus}) species have reported fruiting temperature ranges reaching 28 to 30~\textdegree{}C \cite[Table~1, p.~640]{bellettini2019pleurotus} and grow on agricultural residues \cite[Section~2.1, p.~635]{bellettini2019pleurotus}.

The evidence therefore falls into three groups. Fruit vegetables, leafy greens, mushrooms and seed potatoes are produced commercially in controlled environments. Ware potato, wheat, soybean and peanut have been grown to harvest in research facilities. For sweet potato, cassava and yam, no controlled-environment yield data were found among the sources reviewed.

\subsection{Nutrients, not only calories}\label{sec:nutrition-nutrients}

Energy is one of many requirements. This section uses the FAO/WHO recommended nutrient intakes for vitamins and minerals \cite{faowho2004vitmin} and the EFSA population reference intake for protein of 0.83~g per kilogram of body weight per day, derived for protein with a PD-CAAS of 1.0 \cite[Section~3.4, p.~19]{efsa2017drv}. Several requirements cannot be met by choosing crops. No plant has the enzymes to synthesise vitamin B12 \cite[Section~14.1, p.~279]{faowho2004vitmin}. Between latitudes 42\textdegree{}N and 42\textdegree{}S, synthesis in the skin is the most relevant source of vitamin D for most people \cite[p.~53]{faowho2004vitmin}. The iodine and selenium contents of plants depend on the soil in which they grow \cite[p.~305; p.~200]{faowho2004vitmin}. The requirement for iron depends on how much the diet allows to be absorbed: for menstruating women it is 29.4~mg per day at 10\% bioavailability and 58.8~mg at 5\% \cite[Table~13.5, p.~271]{faowho2004vitmin}. For vitamin A the report gives conflicting conversion factors for carotenes, recommending 1:14 for mixed vegetable diets in one place and retaining 1:6 in another \cite[pp.~26, 29]{faowho2004vitmin}.

The minimum area that meets all requirements at once can be found by linear programming: choose the area of each crop so that the total area is smallest while every nutrient constraint is met. Masuda, Tako and Nitta applied this method to the Japanese Closed Ecology Experiment Facilities. With 18 nutrients, Japanese recommended allowances for sedentary men, light levels of 46.3 and 28.3~mol~m$^{-2}$~d$^{-1}$ and vitamins B12 and D supplied as tablets, they obtained 129.14~m$^2$ for two people, which they report as 64.8~m$^2$ per person, or 74.5~m$^2$ with 15\% added for seedlings \cite[pp.~62--64]{masuda2005ceef}. DeFries et al.\ propose a related single-crop metric, the number of adults whose requirement for a nutrient one hectare of a crop can meet in a year \cite[p.~240]{defries2015metrics}.

We applied the linear-programming method to open-field yields (our calculation). With world-average FAOSTAT yields, USDA composition, FAO/WHO intakes for adults aged 19 to 50, iron at 10\% bioavailability and vitamin A at 1:6, the minimum area is 443, 494 and 562~m$^2$ per person at 2{,}100, 2{,}400 and 2{,}800~kcal per day, the same for women and men. The binding constraints are energy, pantothenate and calcium, and the solution uses maize, carrots and spinach. Iron at 5\% bioavailability raises the area for women to 543~m$^2$; best national yields lower it to 237~m$^2$; limiting each crop to a quarter of energy intake gives 538 to 542~m$^2$. Including all FAO/WHO upper limits makes the problem infeasible because of the magnesium limit of 350~mg per day, which the report gives for intake ``from foods and drinking water'' in the same section in which it describes dietary magnesium as relatively innocuous \cite[p.~225]{faowho2004vitmin}; without that limit the result is unchanged. Vitamin B12, vitamin D, iodine, selenium and biotin are not constrained. Applied to the five crops measured in the NASA chamber \cite[Table~2, p.~711]{wheeler2008crop}, the same method gives 41.6 to 51.5~m$^2$ of growing area per person, mostly potato with lettuce and tomato, with lamps using 35 to 47~MWh per year at 4.1~$\mu$mol~J$^{-1}$ (our calculation). These are minimum areas, not diets: they exclude losses, seed, rotation, paths, cooking losses and acceptability.

An urban garden plot shows why energy sets the area. Baldi, Gallo and Lenzi estimated the production of 50~m$^2$ social-garden plots in Prato, Italy, of which about 30~m$^2$ were cultivated and 81\% of that with tomato; the yields were not measured but computed from Italian average yields \cite[pp.~2--3, 5; Fig.~4, p.~7]{baldi2024garden}. Against Italian reference values for people over 75, they report that one plot covers 59 to 73\% of one person's vitamin~C, 20 to 24\% of vitamin~A and 2.9\% of calcium \cite[Table~4, p.~9]{baldi2024garden}. Recomputing the same plot against FAO/WHO intakes for adults with USDA composition data (our calculation), it covers about 97\% of vitamin~C, 41 to 50\% of vitamin~A, 3\% of calcium, 4 to 5\% of protein and 2\% of energy. A vegetable plot of this size can supply a large part of some vitamins and almost none of the energy, and the energy requirement is what sets the hundreds of square metres in the open-field result.

\subsection{From bounds to a per-user answer}\label{sec:nutrition-peruser}

The quantity the project needs for each user is the same linear program with site-specific coefficients: for each candidate crop, its yield and its energy cost at that site, daylit or lit, and the user's own requirements. The objective can be the smallest energy use, or a weighted combination of energy and area, subject to the nutrient constraints. Because area and energy pull against each other, the result is a set of trade-off points rather than one optimum. The proposed solution (Section~\ref{sec:solution}) describes the system intended to compute it.

The coefficients have to be estimated with crop models, and the evidence on how well such models transfer between sites is mixed. A greenhouse tomato model run with a single set of parameters taken from the literature simulated dry-matter yield in Dutch and Spanish greenhouses with fair accuracy, underestimating the low-technology Spanish case by about 10\%, but it overestimated growth-chamber yields by 33\% on average \cite[Sections~5.1--5.3, pp.~390--391]{vanthoor2011tomato}. Its authors recommend calibrating the temperature, photosynthesis and fruit-growth parameters for each cultivar \cite[Section~5.5, p.~393]{vanthoor2011tomato}, and in their model the harvest rate at a 24-hour mean of 26~\textdegree{}C is 19.6\% of that at 18~\textdegree{}C \cite[Section~5.4, p.~391]{vanthoor2011tomato}. In a comparison of 27 wheat models, simulated yields spread widely when the modellers had no access to the observed data, and after calibration against those same data more than half of the simulated yields fell within the mean variation of field experiments; the uncertainty grew with temperature, and no model had been tested with elevated CO$_2$ and high temperature together \cite[pp.~827, 829]{asseng2013uncertainty}. In a calibration exercise with 29 modelling groups, the authors found no consensus on how to calibrate crop models, even for one model structure and one dataset, and stressed the importance of default parameter values \cite[pp.~7--8]{wallach2021chaos}.

What can be shared across sites, on this evidence, is the model structure, default parameter values and calibration tools. The parameters that depend on cultivar and site have to be calibrated from local measurements, which is the role the measurements of each unit would play. A per-user result is therefore an estimate with an uncertainty that the calibration reduces, not an exact figure.
