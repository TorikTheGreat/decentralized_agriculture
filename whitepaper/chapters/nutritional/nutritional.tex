\section{Food self-sufficiency as a computable quantity}\label{sec:nutrition-analysis}

A natural question to ask of any food-production system is whether it can make its user food-independent. For the platform proposed here, the honest answer is that this is not a single fact to be asserted but a quantity each user can compute for their own circumstances. How much of a household's food a unit supplies depends on the crops grown, the floor area and daylight available, the surrounding climate, and the energy the user is able to provide. What can be established in advance are the physical bounds within which that computation falls, and the mechanism by which the system turns those bounds into a specific answer for a specific place. This section sets out both, and in doing so replaces the question ``can a greenhouse feed me?'' with the more useful ``how much, at what cost in area and energy, here?''.

\subsection{The area bound: calories are limited by photosynthesis, not by crop choice}

The first bound is set by physics rather than by engineering. An adult requires on the order of 2{,}000~kcal per day, or roughly 730{,}000~kcal per year.% SOURCE NEEDED: FAO/WHO reference energy requirement.
Dividing one by the other, full caloric self-sufficiency on the best available crops requires something like 200 to 500~square metres of sunlit growing surface per adult, before accounting for storage and processing losses. This figure is not a limitation of controlled-environment agriculture specifically; it is a property of sunlight and leaves, and it holds for a field, a garden, or a greenhouse alike. It is also why crop selection alone cannot deliver caloric independence in a small footprint: energy-dense crops such as oilseeds and groundnuts carry more calories per kilogram than tubers, but not more calories per square metre, because the same photosynthetic ceiling binds them. Their value, developed below, lies elsewhere.

\subsection{Trading area for energy: stacking and the role of light}

The area bound applies to a single sunlit plane. It can be escaped, but only by paying in energy. If growing surfaces are stacked in layers and each layer is lit artificially, the growing area per unit of floor is multiplied by the number of layers, and the footprint can in principle be made as small as desired. The constraint does not disappear; it is converted from an area constraint into an energy constraint, close to one for one, because every layer must be supplied with the light that sunlight would otherwise provide for free.

The magnitude of that energy is large. Measured vertical-farming systems convert only about 1 to 2\% of input energy into stored food calories \cite[Abstract]{lovat2025vf}. Since an adult's annual caloric requirement corresponds to roughly 850~kWh of food energy, meeting it entirely through stacked, artificially lit production would demand on the order of tens of thousands of kilowatt-hours of electricity per adult per year at current efficiencies, several times the total electricity use of a typical household. Vertically farmed staple crops are correspondingly reported to be economically viable only under electricity prices and conversion efficiencies one to two orders of magnitude better than today's \cite[Abstract]{lovat2025vf}. Full caloric independence by stacking is therefore theoretically possible given sufficient energy, but the energy required for the fully stacked, fully lit case is the dominant cost and the binding practical question.

\subsection{The hybrid operating point}

Because area and energy trade against each other, a real installation is best understood as sitting somewhere on a spectrum between two extremes. At one end, a daylit ground-level greenhouse uses free sunlight for photosynthesis and spends energy only on heating, pumps and control; it is inexpensive to run but pays for calories in land. At the other, a stacked and lit system is compact but expensive in energy. The efficient design is a hybrid: grow the bulk-calorie crops in the daylit, low-energy mode where floor area is available, and reserve stacking and artificial light for the high-value, low-calorie crops whose value per unit of energy justifies it.

A single practical observation makes this more achievable than it first appears. The generating surface need not be the growing surface. At industrial scale, placing photovoltaics on a greenhouse roof competes with the crop's need for light, which is why dedicated greenhouse operations struggle to power themselves on-site. At household scale that conflict typically vanishes: a home commonly has a small daylit yard suitable for growing and a separate roof, unusable for plants, that is well suited to solar panels. Decoupling the two surfaces is the ordinary residential case, and it removes at the domestic scale a problem that is genuinely hard at the industrial one.

\subsection{A crop portfolio for a resilient calorie and nutrient base}

No crop escapes the caloric ceiling of Section~\ref{sec:nutrition-analysis}, but crops differ in what else they contribute, and a resilient local supply combines them by role rather than choosing among them. Tubers such as potatoes and sweet potatoes provide the highest bulk calories per unit area and can be processed into storable flours and starches. Oilseed legumes and groundnuts (peanuts, soybeans, sunflower) carry the greatest caloric density per kilogram and yield storable oils and protein-rich flours, complementing the tubers on macronutrient balance and shelf life even though they do not exceed them per unit area. Mushrooms provide protein on waste substrate and require no light, sidestepping the energy cost that dominates every other crop. Micronutrient-dense vegetables, herbs and fruits round out the portfolio and are the crops for which the transport, water and pesticide advantages of local controlled production are largest.

The feasibility of these crops in controlled environments varies, and the section should be read with that gradient in mind: leafy greens, tomatoes, tubers and mushrooms are well established; dry beans and soybeans have existing controlled-environment research; groundnuts and other oilseeds are plausible but largely unproven and represent research targets for the project rather than demonstrated capabilities.

\subsection{From bounds to a per-user answer}

The physical bounds above are general; the answer any given user needs is specific. The mechanism that turns one into the other is the data the system is built to generate. For each crop, the shared growth models describe how it converts environmental conditions into yield, a relationship that is a property of the plant and transfers across locations. Combined with a user's own local climate and configuration, the daylit area available, the photovoltaic capacity of the roof, the willingness to stack and light, these models yield a concrete estimate: for this household, in this place, this fraction of its food can be produced at this cost in area and energy. Food self-sufficiency thus ceases to be a slogan to be defended or debunked and becomes a calculation each user can run, which is the only form in which the question has a truthful answer.
